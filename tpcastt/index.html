01

TP-CASTTFormal poetry analysis · Oct 26

Seven letters, seven moves. Same idea as the 7-step read from last week, packed into one word you can remember on a test.

  1. T · Title. Before reading, look at the title and predict what the poem is about.
  2. P · Paraphrase. Put it in your own words, line by line, on the literal level. Find the complete thoughts (poets flip sentence order) and look up words you don't know.
  3. C · Connotation. Now go past literal. Look for figurative language, imagery and sound.
  4. A · Attitude / Tone. How does the speaker feel? Humour? Sarcasm? Awe?
  5. S · Shifts. Where does the speaker or the attitude change? Clues: key words, a jump in time, punctuation.
  6. T · Title (again). Look at it a second time, now on an interpretive level. What does it mean now that you've read the poem?
  7. T · Theme. Say what the poem is about (the subject), then what the poet is saying about it (the theme).
Sound familiar?The last T is Unit 1's formula: Theme = Topic + Message. "Death" is a subject. What the poet says about death is the theme.
02

Watch it workA practice poem, not one from class

"Fire and Ice" · Robert Frost (1920)

Some say the world will end in fire,
Some say in ice.
From what I've tasted of desire
I hold with those who favor fire.
But if it had to perish twice,
I think I know enough of hate
To say that for destruction ice
Is also great
And would suffice.

  1. Title: two opposites, both deadly. Prediction: a fight between heat and cold? Something destructive.
  2. Paraphrase: People argue the world will end by burning or by freezing. From my experience of wanting things, I'd bet on fire. But if the world had to be destroyed a second time, I know enough about hatred to say ice would work just as well.
  3. Connotation: fire = desire (hot, consuming). Ice = hate (cold, hard). The world isn't really burning; people are. "Tasted" makes desire something you swallow. Rhymes chain together (fire, desire, fire / twice, ice, suffice), and the last lines get shorter and shorter, like the end closing in.
  4. Tone: calm, almost casual, about the end of the world. "Would suffice" is a shrug. That coolness is what makes it chilling.
  5. Shift: line 5, at "But." The speaker turns from fire to ice, and from desire to hate.
  6. Title again: not weather. Fire and ice are two human emotions that can wreck everything.
  7. Theme: subject: human emotion and destruction. Theme: uncontrolled desire and cold hatred are both powerful enough to destroy the world.
NoticeThe theme is a full sentence, it's arguable, and you can prove it with lines from the poem. That's the bar.
03

Where people slipQuick fixes

Paraphrase is literal. Only literal.

No meaning yet, no symbols. Just what's happening. Meaning comes in the C step.

Flipped sentencesPoets reorder words to fit rhythm or rhyme. "Whose woods these are I think I know" (Frost again) → "I think I know whose woods these are." Unflip it first.

Tone ≠ mood

Tonethe speaker's attitude. A step in TP-CASTT.
Moodhow you feel reading it.

Name tone with real adjectives: bitter, wistful, mocking, reverent, detached. "Sad" and "happy" are the weakest answers you can give.

Shift hunters

  • Turn words: but, yet, however, still, although, now.
  • Time jumps: past to present, night to day.
  • Punctuation: a dash, a colon, a question, a new stanza.
  • Who's talking: "I" becomes "we," or a new voice cuts in.

The Oct 26 poemsTwo dark ones. The TP-CASTT is yours.

"Because I could not stop for Death" · Emily Dickinson

American, written in the 1860s and published after her death. A speaker remembers a carriage ride in which Death shows up as a polite gentleman caller.

Read it on Poetry Foundation.

"The Hollow Men" · T. S. Eliot

Published in 1925, in the wreckage after World War I. Spoken by a "we," a group of men who are empty inside. It ends on one of the most quoted last lines in English poetry.

Read it on Poets.org.

Your turn

Run all seven letters on one of these, in your notebook. Start with just T and P; those two unlock everything else.

Connection to chase: "Fire and Ice" and "The Hollow Men" were both written within five years of each other, and both are about how the world ends. Do they agree?

05

The Rime of the Ancient MarinerSamuel Taylor Coleridge · Oct 28

One sailor. One bad shot. A lifetime of paying for it.

First published in 1798 and revised by Coleridge until the 1834 version the class is reading. It's long, it's told in short four-line stanzas like an old ballad, and it's split into seven parts. The 1834 text also has little prose notes in the margin that explain what's happening. Use them.

The frame: an old sailor stops a young man on his way into a wedding, grabs him with his "glittering eye," and won't let go until he's told his story. The story inside is the voyage.

The map

  • I: The Mariner stops the Wedding-Guest. His ship is driven south into ice. An albatross appears, the ice splits, a good wind follows. He shoots it with his crossbow.
  • II: The crew can't decide if he was right or wrong. Then the wind dies. Stuck, burning, no water to drink. They hang the dead albatross around his neck.
  • III: A ghost ship arrives. On it, Death and Life-in-Death play dice for the crew. The 200 sailors drop dead. Life-in-Death wins the Mariner.
  • IV: Alone with the dead. He can't pray. Then he sees water-snakes in the moonlight and blesses them without meaning to. The albatross falls off his neck into the sea.
  • V: Rain. The dead crew rise and sail the ship, moved by spirits. He hears two voices talking about him.
  • VI: Your group's part. Read it yourself first.
  • VII: Done together in class: how the voyage ends, and why he still has to tell the story.
