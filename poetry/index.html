01

What is poetry?Class 1 · Oct 20

Mr. Winter's starting point: poetry is meant to be felt differently by different readers. There's no single correct way to understand a poem. You need a reading you can back up.

The class opened with "Skyscrapers" by Matt Haig, a tiny poem stacked one or two words per line. Its idea: tip a poem on its side and it looks like a city skyline seen from far away. Skyscrapers made of words.

The point: how a poem looks on the page is part of what it means.

"Poetry is like…" and concrete images

The class read other people's definitions of poetry. Every one uses a concrete image: something real you could google a picture of, used to explain a feeling or a hard idea.

  • An IKEA bed (Carl Leggo): the manual doesn't make sense, it looks simple and is hard to put together.
  • Grandmother's stained recipe cards: poetry is personal, old, about traditions; useful to others before you.
  • A DJ: poetry plays with sounds and remixes other people's ideas.
  • Riding a horse in the rain with no reins: scary, unpredictable, a skill most people don't have yet.
Concrete vs not"A cracked phone screen" is concrete; you can picture it. "Frustration" isn't. Poets explain the second by showing you the first.

02

How to read a poemTake notes on this. Mr. Winter shows it every class.

  1. Read the title. Anything interesting there?
  2. Look at the poem. Anything interesting about how it looks on the page? Line length, shape, capitals, gaps.
  3. Read for WORD meaning. Understand the words themselves. Look up the ones you don't know.
  4. Read for PHRASE meaning. Where's the punctuation? Who is speaking? What's the tone and the mood?
  5. Read again for SOUNDS. Anything interesting when you hear it?
  6. Read again for POETIC DEVICES and IMAGERY. Figurative language, symbols, alliteration, onomatopoeia.
  7. What's the main message? What do you think the poem is saying?

Tone vs mood

ToneHow the speaker feels about the poem's subject.
MoodHow you, the reader, feel while reading.
ExampleA speaker describes a storm calmly and with wonder (tone: awed, calm), but the details of the drowning boat make you anxious (mood: tense). They can be different.

The speaker isn't the poet

Same rule as narrators in Unit 1. The "I" in a poem is a voice the poet created. It can be an animal, an object, anyone. That matters for the poems below.

03

Denotation vs connotationClass 2–3 · Oct 21–22 · The worksheet lesson

Words are the building blocks of poetry, and words that mean the "same" thing (synonyms) rarely feel the same.

DenotationThe literal, dictionary meaning. No feelings attached.
ConnotationThe suggested extra meaning: the feeling a word carries.

Connotation comes from culture and context. You need to be able to say whether it's positive or negative.

Mr. Winter's example"Obese" and "portly" both denote overweight. "Obese" suggests poor health and unpleasantness. "Portly" suggests dignity.

Same word, different feeling

Context can flip a word's connotation. The handout uses "youthful" (denotation: seeming young):

  • Admiring an old woman's youthful energy → positive: full of life.
  • An argument with too much youthful enthusiasm and no common sense → negative: immature.

Spot the feeling

Each pair means roughly the same thing. Which one would you rather be called?

thriftycareful with money
cheapwon't spend a cent
confidentbelieves in himself
cockya little too much
curiouswants to know
nosyin your business
homewarmth, family
housejust the building

"House" is close to pure denotation: most people feel nothing about it. "Home" is loaded.

04

Why it mattersThe link between words and imagery

Most poets don't make direct statements, because direct statements don't hit your emotions. Instead they paint pictures with connotatively powerful words that make you see, hear, smell, feel and empathize.

Those pictures are called imagery: sensory details. Same definition as Unit 1: the five senses, using adjectives and figurative language.

Watch it work"The dog was old" tells you. "The dog's grey muzzle rested on my shoe" makes you feel it. Every word choice nudges the mood.

Poetic devices = figurative + sound

Mr. Winter splits poetic devices into two families. You already know a lot of the first one from Unit 1:

  • Figurative: personification, metaphor, extended metaphor, symbols, allusion…
  • Sound: alliteration, onomatopoeia and more. This is the next lesson.
05

The class poemsKnow what happens in each. The meaning part is yours.

"Jaguar" · Francisco X. Alarcón

Speaker: the jaguar itself, talking about the jungle park it lives in.

What happens: some people say the jaguar is almost extinct in the park. The jaguar says those people don't realize it's all around them: the scent of the orchids is its scent, the roar of the waterfalls is its ancestors' roar, the stars at night are the spots on its fur. It ends by claiming it is, and always will be, the wild spirit of the jungle.

On the page: very short lines, often two or three words, and almost no punctuation.

"sturgeon" · Kateri Akiwenzie-Damm

A Canadian Indigenous writer. The sturgeon is a large, ancient river fish.

Speaker: a person who says that whenever a sturgeon is caught in the Rainy River, she feels it happen to her: gasping for air, strange hands, the struggle to be free, the blow to the head.

What happens: even later, sitting at her kitchen table, she knows this feeling. That's why she doesn't eat sturgeon. By the end she says she is a sturgeon.

On the page: all lowercase, even the title and "i." No capital letters at all.

"The Promise" · Jane Hirshfield
