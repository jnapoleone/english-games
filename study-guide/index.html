The 3 StoriesThe game uses these. Know what happens.

STORY 1

“Lather and Nothing Else” · Hernando Téllez

What happens: A barber is shaving Captain Torres, the army officer who hunts and kills rebels. The barber is secretly a rebel spy. With a razor at Torres's throat, he agonizes: should he kill him? He decides he's a barber, not a murderer, and finishes a perfect shave. As Torres leaves, he says:

“They told me that you'd kill me. I came to find out.”

The twist: Torres knew the barber was a rebel the whole time. The barber thought he had the power. Torres actually did.

Setting: A barbershop in a Spanish-speaking country at war. Mr. Winter connected it to Colombia's La Violencia (1948–1958), when over 200,000 people died.

Told by: the barber, using “I” (first person). We only know what he knows, which is why the ending shocks us.

STORY 2

“The Necklace” · Guy de Maupassant

What happens: Mathilde Loisel is pretty but poor, married to a clerk at the Ministry of Education in Paris, and miserable because she dreams of luxury. Her husband gets an invitation to a fancy Ministry ball. He gives up the 400 francs he saved for a rifle so she can buy a dress, and she borrows a diamond necklace from her rich friend, Madame Forestier.

She's the star of the ball, then loses the necklace on the way home. Instead of admitting it, they buy a replacement for 36,000 francs, using his inheritance and huge loans. They spend ten years in poverty paying it off. Mathilde becomes old, rough and hard-working.

The twist: She finally tells Madame Forestier, who says:

“Oh, my poor, poor Mathilde! Mine was false. It was worth five hundred francs at the most!”

Setting: Paris, France, in the late 1800s (the Ministry, the Champs-Élysées, francs).

STORY 3

“When the Jade Peony” · Wayson Choy

What happens: Told by Sek-Lung, age 8, the youngest in a Chinese-Canadian family in Vancouver's Chinatown. His Grandmama (83) makes beautiful wind chimes from glass she finds in back alleys, which embarrasses the rest of the family. Together they build one last, sacred wind chime for her death, so her spirit can say goodbye.

Her treasure is a jade peony pendant from a pale juggler she loved when she was young. When she sees a white cat with pink eyes, she knows it's a sign she'll die soon. After she dies, her son raises the chime, and Sek-Lung finds the jade peony in his pocket.

Big ideas: symbols (wind chime, jade peony, white cat) and the clash between old-country ways and being “Canadian now.”

Plot & SettingGame: Stage 1

The rule

PlotThe shape of a story: the events in order.
ClimaxThe turning point, where the tension is highest.
SettingPLACE + TIME. Give as much important info as you can.

The plot mountain:

Expo­sition
Rising action
CLIMAX
Falling action
Reso­lution

In the stories

  • Mr. Winter's example of a strong setting: “Rural France, a castle, 18th century.” Place AND time, with detail.
  • “The Necklace”: Paris, France, late 1800s. You infer it from clues like the Champs-Élysées and francs.
  • “The Jade Peony”: Vancouver's Chinatown (Keefer and Pender Streets).

Watch out

“In a city” or “long ago” is too vague. Always give place AND time.

What comes right after the rising action?
The climax.
What two things make up setting?
Place and time.

Character TypesGame: Stage 2

The rule: 3 pairs

ProtagonistThe main character.
AntagonistOpposes the protagonist and creates conflict.
StaticDoes NOT change by the end.
DynamicDOES change by the end.
FlatSimple, one-sided (and static).
RoundComplex and realistic, many sides.

Inference + “show, don't tell”

Inference = using facts, observations and reasoning to reach a conclusion. In other words, a guess using evidence.

Good writers show, don't tell. Instead of writing “the barber was nervous,” Téllez shows it:

“My hands began to tremble.” · “Very pale.” · “My shirt soaked with sweat.”

Mr. Winter's model answer: these reactions show the barber is terrified of killing. He is not a hardened killer.

Torres hangs up his gun belt, closes his eyes, and talks about hanging rebels like it's the weather. That shows he is brave and calm, and that violence is routine for him.

In the stories

  • Mathilde changes over ten years of hard work, so she's dynamic. She's also complex, so she's round.
  • Mr. Loisel giving up his rifle money shows his character without the author saying “he was generous.”
A character who changes by the end is…
Dynamic.
What's the difference between an antagonist and a protagonist?
The protagonist is the main character. The antagonist opposes them and creates conflict.

ConflictGame: Stage 3 · Definitions are on the test

ON THE TEST / EXAM

The rule

Conflict = any opposition the main character faces. It moves the events along. It's not just fights! Grade 9 short stories usually have one main conflict.

vs. CharacterStruggles against an antagonist.
vs. FateStruggles against fate or the circumstances of life.
vs. SocietyStruggles against the ideas, practices or customs of other people.
vs. SelfStruggles with their own soul, right vs. wrong, limits, choices.
vs. EnvironmentStruggles against their (usually natural) environment.

Memory trick

Ask: who or what is in the way? Another person → Character. Destiny → Fate. The rules or customs of a group → Society. Their own mind → Self. Nature → Environment.

In the stories: “Lather” has all three of these

  • Barber vs. Captain Torres → Character vs. Character
  • Barber torn over whether to kill → Character vs. Self
  • Barber vs. a violent political regime → Character vs. Society

Bonus from the myths: King Acrisius tries to escape the oracle's prophecy in the Perseus story → Character vs. Fate.

A hiker is trapped in a blizzard. Which conflict?
Character vs. Environment.
Someone fights against their town's customs. Which conflict?
Character vs. Society.
Can you name all 5 without looking?
Character, Fate, Society, Self, Environment.

Point of ViewGame: Stage 4

The rule

POV = the angle the story is told from. The narrator (the voice telling the story) is NOT the author!

First person“I.” We see through one character's eyes and only know what they know. Can be reliable or unreliable.
Third person omniscient“He/she.” A god's-eye view: knows and sees everything.
Third person limited“He/she.” Knows everything about ONE character.

In the stories

  • “Lather”: first person (the barber). We can't see inside Torres's head, so his last line is a shock. POV controls what the reader knows.
  • “The Jade Peony”: first person (Sek-Lung remembering).
  • “The Necklace”: third person (“she,” “he”).
  • Mr. Winter's examples: To Kill a Mockingbird (first person, the main character) and Sherlock Holmes (first person, a witness).
Who wrote “Lather,” and who narrates it?
Hernando Téllez wrote it. The barber narrates it.
A narrator who knows everyone's thoughts is…
Third person omniscient.

ThemeGame: Stage 5 · Mr. Winter cares about this one a lot

The rule

Theme = an insight about human life that the story reveals. The author almost never says it directly, so you have to infer it.

THEME = TOPIC + MESSAGE

  • Topic = the big idea (ambition, power, friendship).
  • Message = what the author says ABOUT it.

Watch out: theme is NOT…

  • One word. “Friendship” is a topic, not a theme.
  • The conflict, the moral, the topic, or a simple fact.
  • A Hallmark card or fortune cookie (“Friends are always there for you”). Dig deeper!

Build one in 3 steps

  • 1. Find the topic. Check the title, look for repetition, notice figurative language, and ask what big idea the story deals with.
  • 2. Try a “versus.” Love vs. hate. Appearance vs. reality.
  • 3. Say what the story says about it. Your message must be arguable (someone could disagree), provable (with evidence from the story) and applicable (it works for other stories and life).

Mr. Winter's examples

  • Macbeth: “Unchecked ambition corrupts the human soul.”
  • Shrek: “Only those willing to face the ugliest parts of themselves are able to find true love.”
  • Harry Potter: “To become a powerful leader, it is necessary to break some rules.”
  • Black Mirror: “Humans who rely too much on technology lose their humanity.”
  • “Lather”: topics are strength vs. violence and appearance vs. reality. Themes: “Reason can overtake the instinct to be violent.” / “True power is rarely where it appears to be.”
Someone says the theme is “Bravery.” What's wrong?
It's one word, so it's only a topic. Add a message: what does the story say about bravery?
What are the 3 tests for a good theme statement?
Arguable, provable, applicable.

Boss: Twists & IronyGame: Boss stage

The rule

Situational ironyThe outcome is the exact OPPOSITE of what you expect.
ForeshadowingHints the author plants early about what's coming.

Both stories end in situational irony

  • “Lather”: we expect the barber holds all the power because he has the razor. Instead, Torres knew all along and was testing him.
  • “The Necklace”: ten years of poverty to replace a diamond necklace… that was fake, worth 500 francs at most.

Numbers the boss asks about

400 francsMr. Loisel's rifle money, spent on the dress.
36,000 francsCost of the replacement necklace.
18,000 francsMr. Loisel's inheritance (the rest was borrowed).
10 yearsTime it took to pay off the debt.
500 francsWhat the original necklace was really worth.

Spot the foreshadowing yourself

“The Necklace” hides hints about the twist. Reread the scene where Mathilde borrows the necklace, and the scene where she returns it. How does Madame Forestier treat her jewellery? That's your clue.

What is situational irony?
When the outcome is the exact opposite of what you expect.
What did Torres know before he sat down?
That the barber was a rebel who might kill him. He came to find out.
